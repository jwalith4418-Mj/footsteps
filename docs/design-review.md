# Which rules should change before the next paper game?

**Use base-1 for the first four-person paper session. Prepare one comparison: delay nominations until the end of the first circuit of builders.** Early removal has the largest demonstrated cost to participation. The evidence for changing the floor, clock, or movement vote is weaker. This is a decision about what to test next. We have measured what several policies can do on these boards; we still need people to tell us when suspicion, an irreversible move, or sitting out makes the game more or less enjoyable.

## 1. Give early elimination the first comparison

On deal 8101, P2 voted against the opening step toward EXIT. P0 nominated P1, who had supported the step. P0 and P2 removed P1. P1 never built and missed the nine remaining group turns. With the same deal and a more cautious observer, P1 stayed and the team reached EXIT on turn eight. Across an earlier panel all 16 games saw someone removed on turn one. At the other extreme, the two fully model-played receipt games produced 54 passes in 54 nomination windows. These are examples of the outcomes the procedure admits. Model prompts can move between those extremes, so their frequency at a human table is open. [Early removal](../experiments/social/fresh-base/8101-red2.json) · [Matched deal](../experiments/social/fresh-ledger/8101-red2.json) · [Earlier panel](../experiments/model-panel/results.csv) · [Receipt games](../experiments/social/README.md)

The strongest reason to keep opening nominations is that removal can prevent an untrusted builder from making a permanent placement. The strongest reason to delay them is that a builder's face, rotation and proposal give others something to argue about, and a player recruited to play a tile game gets to lay a tile. A cap of one removal per round would prevent a cascade but would still let P1 sit out that entire deal. Conversely, removing elimination altogether would leave the Blue seats with fewer ways to respond to persistent sabotage.

For a comparison session use one explicit variation: **on turns one through three of a four-seat game, skip nominations. Starting immediately after movement on turn four, use the ordinary nomination round and all subsequent base-1 rules.** Keep movement votes, tie breaks and secret roles throughout. With the printed exit six steps away, all four seats get to build before ordinary movement could end the game. The cost is equally concrete: the Red seat also gets its first build even if three suspicious players want to act sooner. Prefer this variant if early removal at human tables costs someone their only meaningful decision and the delayed round still leaves people with a consequential response. Retain immediate nominations if people can articulate an urgent, preventable threat before the last opening build and value that choice.

## 2. Keep the floor and clock for this comparison

Four rotating cooperative builders using the printed kit and the existing eight-turn budget reached (4, 2) in 198/200 deals with the exits policy and 184/200 with the route policy. All 198 exits wins took six turns. Only 74 of the 184 route wins did. This gives the group a genuine construction task, room for recovery, and a reason to be cautious about calibrating suspense from one policy's finish times. A three-seat game with the same kit instead gets ten turns; one Blue removal also turns a 2–1 Blue majority into a two-seat table where the builder settles a tied movement ballot. The four-seat paper setting differs on both counts. [Four-seat results](../experiments/offset-counts.md) · [Setup analysis](../experiments/exit-offsets.md) · [Paper kit](../playtest/README.md)

Keep the separate open START and EXIT, irreversible placements, and rotation for the baseline. A plausible trap is evidence worth debating, and a Blue player may have only unappealing faces in hand. Changing orientation, tile mix and turn margin together would obscure which of those produced a helpless build. Revisit the mix or margin if cooperating human builders repeatedly know what they want to do and the available tiles or remaining turns prevent it. Revisit the presentation first if players had a viable route but misread its entrance and exit. The printed-orientation question has its own comparison. [Rotation question](https://github.com/othercriteria/footsteps/issues/54)

## 3. Keep ballot rules, hidden roles and posthumous wins

The movement vote and elimination vote serve different purposes. A two-seat split can still move the expedition on the builder's ballot. Removing someone from that same table requires both votes, including the nominee's. That distinction preserves an active game for the two survivors. An actual accusation can still change a listener's vote, and writing down who voted which way makes a disputed claim answerable. Named choices and an accurate vote receipt changed model behavior on identical positions. A high rate of yes votes or self-vetoes alone gives little reason to change either threshold. A short public tally of proposal, destination and named votes is available to a facilitator using the existing rules. Avoid labeling a dissent as sabotage; the direct step can be a trap. [Matched ballots](../experiments/ballots/README.md) · [Accusation trials](../experiments/social/README.md)

In two earlier three-seat games the Red seat went out on turn one and one Blue builder eventually played alone. One builder reached EXIT on the last draw and the other team's expedition ran out of time. Ending immediately on removal of the last Red seat would erase that distinction and publicly confirm the removed player's role. Retain the hidden role and settlement at EXIT or depletion. Revisit the procedure if human players can already see how the game must end and consistently experience the remaining turns as bookkeeping. ## Two questions for the facilitator

1. **When did you first want somebody out, and what would you have prevented?** Mark that moment even if nobody nominates. Record the public action players cited, whether the suspected player had built, the ballot, and the actual time anyone spent sitting out. Ask the eliminated player privately whether they had a meaningful choice before leaving. Save role and hand explanations for after the game.
2. **When did you think the route was won or lost, and did anyone still have a choice that mattered?** Capture the floor, marker, turn and remaining bag at that moment. After play, distinguish a sealed position, insufficient turns, a difficult private hand, and a route the table overlooked. Ask whether playing those remaining turns was interesting.

Use the normal kit first. If running the opening-protection comparison, keep seat count, tile mix, exit and post-deal bag size the same. Use fresh shuffled deals so people cannot infer hidden hands or roles from the previous game. With multiple groups, alternate which rule they learn first. Mark any variation on the observer sheet. Existing replay IDs describe the adjudicated base rules; implementing opening protection would need an explicit rules variant and worked replay before it became a supported digital setting.
